# Vegan Coffee

by Marlon Barrios Solano

His mother was pregnant with him when they left Venezuela.

He knew this because she told the story often enough that it settled into him like memory. Not as trauma, not as nostalgia—more like a fact that explained everything else.

They left during the long unraveling.  
Chavismo.  
Shortages.  
Queues that moved slower than time.

They didn’t flee dramatically. There were no sirens, no soldiers. Just the quiet realization that staying meant shrinking. His mother already understood this. She always understood things first.

They arrived in Miami with relatives who helped and watched at the same time. For two years they lived in the same house—too many bodies, too many opinions, everyone working something different. Cleaning. Driving. Selling. Translating.

Sebastián learned later that those years were hard.

He remembered only fragments.

Raised voices at night.  
Doors closing too hard.  
The television always on.

By the time he was four or five, the tension had settled into something quieter and more permanent.

Divorce came without ceremony.

His father drifted through jobs—construction, sales, something logistical—until eventually, somehow, insurance. Sebastián didn’t fully understand what his father did now. He only knew it sounded stable when people asked.

His mother, on the other hand, always had more money.

Not extravagance.  
Control.

She worked in real estate. She understood timing, neighborhoods before they were named trends. She bought carefully. Sold early. She knew when to wait and when to move.

Sebastián respected her deeply.

Loved her.

She was close in a way that felt intentional. Always calling. Always advising. Always watching the horizon before anyone else noticed it changing.

And she always said the same thing:

*You have to know what you want.*  
*Tienes que saber lo que quieres.*

It wasn’t pressure.  
It was instruction.

It was January 2026, and it was cold in Gainesville.

Cold in a way Miami never was. Dry. Clarifying. Like the air itself was asking questions.

Sebastián had just arrived.

North Florida felt provisional, like a place people passed through while deciding who they were allowed to become. He was starting his master’s degree at the University of Florida—marketing. Practical. Predictable. A discipline that promised continuity.

He wanted stability.

He had listened too closely to stories of rupture.

He was sitting in a café called Karma Cream, next to his building.

A vegan coffee shop.

Outside, electric scooters zipped past constantly—silent, fast, unowned. People mounted and dismounted them without ceremony, as if movement itself had become temporary.

The word *vegan* still felt abstract to him, like a lifestyle that assumed choice. He had never been to one before.

He ordered a cappuccino.

Vegan.

The barista didn’t hesitate.

The drink tasted… fine. Not bad. Just unfamiliar. Like a version of something he thought he already understood.

Behind him, a conversation rose above the café murmur.

One voice was older. Calm. Certain.

“…the mistake,” the man was saying, “is thinking AI replaces labor the way machines replaced muscle. This replaces cognition. Strategy. Pattern recognition.”

Sebastián stopped moving.

“Marketing will go,” the man continued. “Consulting. Analysis. Anything that mistakes thinking for ownership.”

Sebastián felt his stomach tighten.

“That’s why it’s not about jobs anymore,” the man said. “It’s about learning to work inside an ecology of intelligences. Human, machine, hybrid.”

His mother’s voice surfaced, uninvited:

*You should do something with AI.*

He had nodded every time.  
He had done nothing.

Behind him, the man said another name.

“Maduro. Kidnapped. Him and his wife.”

Sebastián felt a rush of heat.

He remembered his mother telling him, sounding relieved, almost happy.

And Trump.

He hated Trump with a clarity that surprised him.

He thought of friends whose parents had lost TPS. Temporary suddenly meaning *over*.

Outside, scooters kept moving.

Inside, the man said, “This isn’t science fiction. It’s infrastructure.”

Something cracked.

Sebastián finished his cappuccino slowly.

It wasn’t bad.

Just different.

He stepped outside, crossed the short distance to his studio apartment, reached into his pocket.

No keys.

Backpack.  
Jacket.

Nothing.

The door was locked.

Sebastián stood there, suddenly aware of how provisional safety had always been.

The coffee shop hummed behind him. Scooters passed. People kept moving.

The future, he realized, had already changed the locks.

And standing there, locked out of the room he thought would hold him steady, he understood:

Knowing what you want might begin exactly here.

With not being able to go back inside.
